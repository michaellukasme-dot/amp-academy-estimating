Quick Class

Residential Electrical Estimating

A short, practical class on building a clean residential electrical estimate — plus a working estimator you can use on a real job today. It installs to your device and runs fully offline; nothing you type leaves your phone or laptop.

What you'll get

  • The line-item method: price the job piece by piece instead of guessing a number.
  • How labor & markup turn your parts cost into a customer price.
  • Tax and a clean 50 / 40 / 10 payment schedule.
  • The Estimator Tool — fill it in, print a professional estimate, export a CSV.
This is a method, not a code rule. Prices, markup, and tax here are starting points from a real working spreadsheet — your costs and local tax will differ. Edit everything. An estimate is not a binding quote or contract.
Lesson 01

The line-item method

Good estimates aren't one big guess — they're a list. Every estimate is a table of items, each with a unit cost and a quantity. Multiply and you get the extended cost for that line; add the lines and you get the parts cost for the whole job.

Extended = Unit cost × Quantity.
Example: Breakers at $30 each, 24 of them → 30 × 24 = $720.

Build your item list once (the tool ships with a common residential set — weatherhead, main cable, meter base, panel, breakers, ground, kitchen/bath lines, range, water heater, dryer, A/C, receptacles, switches, lights, EV charger, mini-split, and more), then just set quantities per job. Add custom lines for anything unusual.

Lesson 02

Labor & markup

Your parts cost is what you pay. The customer price has to cover labor, overhead, and profit. The simplest way to handle that is a markup multiplier applied to each line.

Line price = Extended × markup.
A markup of 1.125 adds 12.5%. A markup of 1.5 adds 50%. Set it to match your real labor and margin — many shops are well above 1.5 once labor is fully loaded.
Honest note: the source spreadsheets used markups of 1.1 and 1.125. Those are low if the multiplier is meant to cover labor and margin — treat the default as a placeholder and set your own. The tool makes markup a single editable field.
Lesson 03

Tax & the payment schedule

Add sales tax (where it applies) to the marked-up price, not to your raw parts cost. The tool defaults to 6% — set your local rate.

Total = (sum of line prices) + tax.
Then stage the cash flow so you're never funding the job out of pocket.

A clean schedule

  • 50% deposit — parts to the jobsite.
  • 40% second payment — first day of work.
  • 10% final — upon completion.
Lesson 04

Using the tool

  1. Open Estimator Tool.
  2. Fill the customer block (name, address, phone, email).
  3. Set quantities on the lines you're using; edit unit costs to your prices; add custom lines.
  4. Set your markup and tax rate.
  5. Hit Print for a clean customer estimate (your internal parts cost is hidden on the printout), or Export CSV to keep records.
Everything stays on your device. Install the class (browser → Install) to use it offline on the truck.

About

About this estimate

How the math works. Each line: Parts = unit cost × qty. Line price = Parts × markup. Pricing Estimate = sum of line prices + tax (tax applies to the marked-up price). Payment schedule is 50 / 40 / 10 of the Pricing Estimate.

Note on the source sheets: two of the originals computed the total slightly differently (one added raw parts and the marked-up price, which double-counts). This tool uses the clean model above — the marked-up price already includes labor & margin. Confirm the markup and tax match your business before sending an estimate.
